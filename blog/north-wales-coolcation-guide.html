Why North Wales is the UK's Best Coolcation Destination

Wales is officially the UK's number one destination for "coolcations" in 2026. With hotel searches up 65% year-on-year, here's why Llandudno and Conwy should be your next summer escape.

By Visitor Guide UK Updated October 2026

Summer travel is changing. As heatwaves become more intense across southern Europe, British travellers are looking closer to home for their holidays. The trend is called a "coolcation" — a holiday taken to a cooler destination to escape the heat.

And according to data from Trip.com, Wales is the UK's number one coolcation destination for summer 2026. Searches for Welsh hotels have risen 65% year-on-year, while rail bookings are up 37%. Wales is now outperforming Scotland, Cornwall, Devon, the Lake District, and Yorkshire combined [citation:13][citation:16].

While the whole country is benefiting, two coastal towns in North Wales are seeing particularly strong interest: Llandudno and Conwy [citation:21].

Llandudno: The Queen of Welsh Resorts

Llandudno has been welcoming visitors since the Victorian era. Known as the "Queen of Welsh Resorts" for over 150 years, it attracts nearly 10 million visitors every year — making it the largest and most popular seaside resort in Wales [citation:9].

What makes Llandudno special is how well it has preserved its Victorian character. The town has more Grade II listed buildings than any other seaside town in Wales — an estimated 400 listed structures line the sweeping promenade and seafront [citation:9]. Walking along the wide streets with their cream-and-white terraces feels like stepping back into the golden age of seaside holidays.

Llandudno Pier: The Best in the UK

Stretching 700 metres into the Irish Sea, Llandudno Pier was officially recognised as the best pier in the UK in 2025, having previously won the same title in 2005 [citation:9]. The Grade II listed structure dates back to 1878 and is believed to record over six million visitors annually [citation:9].

The Great Orme and West Shore

Llandudno sits beneath the Great Orme, a dramatic limestone headland that offers spectacular views over the sea. During summer, you can reach the summit by cable car — one of the longest in the UK [citation:16]. On the other side of the headland lies West Shore, a quieter sandy beach that's ideal for swimming and watching the sunset [citation:16].

"Llandudno has preserved its most authentic Victorian buildings. A visit is like stepping back into the golden age of seaside leisure." — Daily Express

Conwy: A Medieval Walled Town

Just 5 km from Llandudno — an 8-minute train journey [citation:3] — lies Conwy, one of the most historic and photogenic towns in North Wales. While Llandudno offers Victorian charm, Conwy delivers medieval drama.

Conwy Castle: 700 Years of History

Built by King Edward I between 1283 and 1287, Conwy Castle is one of the most magnificent medieval fortresses in Europe. It's part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, alongside Edward's other great castles at Beaumaris, Harlech, and Caernarfon [citation:5].

The castle is exceptionally well-preserved. You can climb the restored spiral staircases and walk a complete circuit around the battlements, with views over the harbour, the narrow streets, and the mountains of Eryri (Snowdonia) in the distance [citation:5]. The town walls — an unbroken ring 1.3 km long — still completely enclose Conwy, making it one of the best-preserved walled towns in Britain.

Britain's Smallest House

On Conwy Quay, you'll find a red-painted curiosity: the Smallest House in Great Britain. Measuring just 3.05m by 1.83m, it was last lived in by a fisherman named Robert Jones, who was 1.9m tall [citation:10]. It's a quick but memorable stop.

Plas Mawr and Aberconwy House

For history beyond the castle, visit Plas Mawr, one of the finest Elizabethan townhouses in Britain, or Aberconwy House, the only medieval merchant's house to survive in Conwy [citation:10]. Both offer a glimpse into life in the walled town across different centuries.

Why Wales is Winning the Coolcation Race

So why is Wales outperforming other UK staycation hotspots? The answer is simple: it's cooler.

While southern England and parts of Europe swelter, the North Wales coast typically enjoys temperatures around 18°C in July [citation:23]. That's warm enough for the beach, but cool enough for comfortable walking and sightseeing. Combined with 700-year-old castles, Victorian seaside towns, and easy access to Snowdonia, Wales offers something that few other destinations can match: genuine summer comfort without a long-haul flight.

It's not just British travellers who are noticing. Trip.com found that searches for flights to Cardiff among Spanish users are up nearly 20% year-on-year, with visitors arriving from Malaga, Alicante, and Tenerife [citation:16]. Even people from hotter parts of Europe are choosing Wales for their summer holidays.

Practical Tips for Your North Wales Coolcation

  • Getting there: Llandudno and Conwy are both on the North Wales Coast Line, with direct trains from Chester and Crewe. The journey from London takes around 3 hours with a change at Chester.
  • Getting between the towns: The train from Llandudno to Conwy takes just 8 minutes and runs hourly [citation:3].
  • Best time to visit: Summer (June–August) for the warmest weather and longest days. The Great Orme cable car operates seasonally, usually from Easter to October.
  • Don't miss: Fish and chips on Conwy Quay, a walk around the complete Conwy town walls, and sunset at Llandudno's West Shore.

Is a North Wales Coolcation Worth It?

Absolutely. If you want a UK summer holiday that combines beach time with serious history, Victorian charm with medieval drama, and comfortable temperatures with stunning scenery, Llandudno and Conwy deliver. The data proves that thousands of travellers have already discovered this — and with hotel searches up 65%, you'll want to book before everyone else catches on.
