How to Pay in the UK 2026: Contactless, Oyster (London vs Edinburgh & Manchester) & Tipping

The UK runs almost entirely on contactless payments now, which is good news for most travellers but can catch out anyone expecting to rely on cash. Here's exactly where your card works, where it doesn't, and what else — from Oyster cards to tipping to currency exchange — you need to know before you land.

How Contactless Payment Works in the UK

Contactless payment in the UK uses NFC (Near Field Communication) — the same technology behind Apple Pay and Google Pay. You hold your card, phone or watch close to the reader, and the transaction completes in under a second. No PIN required for amounts up to £100.

In the UK, contactless is accepted almost everywhere: supermarkets, cafes, restaurants, pubs, taxis, buses, the Tube, museums, attractions and most market stalls. If you're visiting from abroad, your contactless Visa, Mastercard or Maestro card will work on UK readers in the vast majority of places.

Contactless limit: The single-transaction limit for contactless cards in the UK is £100. Above that, you'll need to insert the card and enter your PIN. Mobile wallet payments via Apple Pay or Google Pay have no upper limit.

Oyster vs Contactless: Which is Cheaper?

If you're spending any time in London, this is the question that trips up most visitors. Here's the answer, straight:

Bottom line: Oyster and contactless cost exactly the same on the London Underground, buses, Overground and most National Rail services within London. Both apply the same daily and weekly caps automatically. The only difference is that Oyster requires a £7 deposit for the card, while contactless requires nothing extra.

Does Oyster Card Work Outside London?

This is the question that trips up most visitors to the UK. The short answer: Oyster cards only work in London. They are issued by Transport for London (TfL) and are valid on the London Underground, buses, Overground, DLR, Tramlink and most National Rail services within the London travel zones.

Outside London, Oyster cards do not work. If you're travelling to Edinburgh, Manchester, Glasgow, Cardiff, Birmingham or any other UK city, you'll need to use a different payment method.

Does Oyster card work in Edinburgh?

No. Oyster cards do not work in Edinburgh. Edinburgh has its own transport system, run by Lothian Buses and Edinburgh Trams. Here's what you'll need instead:

  • Contactless card or phone: Accepted on all Lothian Buses and Edinburgh Trams. Tap once when boarding a bus, or tap in and out on the tram. The daily fare cap is applied automatically — £5.00 for adults on Lothian Buses, and £5.20 for the tram.
  • Ridacard: A reusable smart card for Edinburgh — similar to London's Oyster but only valid in Edinburgh and the surrounding area. Available from Lothian Buses travel shops.
  • Exact change: Lothian Buses still accept cash, but you must have the exact fare — drivers don't give change. The flat fare is £2.00 for adults.

Bottom line: If you're visiting Edinburgh, don't buy an Oyster card — it won't work. Use contactless instead, which is simpler and capped at the same daily rate.

Does Oyster card work in Manchester?

No. Oyster cards do not work in Manchester. Manchester's public transport (Metrolink trams, buses and trains) is run by Transport for Greater Manchester (TfGM), which has its own payment system — the Bee Network.

  • Contactless card or phone: Accepted on all Bee Network buses and Metrolink trams. Tap in and out on the tram, or tap once when boarding a bus. Daily and weekly caps applied automatically.
  • Bee Network app: For tickets and passes across Manchester's buses and trams.
  • Cash: Accepted on some Manchester buses but not on Metrolink trams, which are cashless.

Bottom line: Oyster won't work in Manchester. Use contactless — it's accepted everywhere on the Bee Network and gets you the same daily caps as a local.

Does Oyster card work in other UK cities?

No. Oyster cards only work in London. Every other UK city has its own transport system:

City Oyster Valid? What to Use Instead
London✅ YesOyster, contactless, or phone
Edinburgh❌ NoContactless, Ridacard, or exact cash
Manchester❌ NoContactless (Bee Network), Bee Network app
Glasgow❌ NoContactless, or buy tickets from the driver
Birmingham❌ NoContactless (Swift card also available)
Liverpool❌ NoContactless, or Walrus card (older system)
Cardiff❌ NoContactless, or buy from the driver
Belfast❌ NoContactless (Translink), or buy tickets
The simplest rule: If you're in London, Oyster and contactless cost the same. If you're anywhere else in the UK, just use contactless — it's accepted on almost every bus, tram and train in the country and automatically applies the same daily and weekly caps as local smart cards.

Should I buy an Oyster card or just use contactless?

For almost every visitor, contactless is simpler and equally cheap. Here's why:

  • No £7 deposit — Oyster requires a £7 refundable deposit; contactless requires nothing.
  • Same fares — both Oyster and contactless apply the same daily and weekly caps on London transport.
  • Works everywhere — contactless works in every UK city; Oyster works only in London.
  • One card for everything — the same contactless card you use for coffee also works on the Tube, buses, trams and most trains.

The only case for buying an Oyster card is if you don't have a contactless card, or if you'd prefer not to have your bank statement filled with small transport charges. For everyone else, contactless is the way to go.

Where Your Card Works — and Where It Doesn't

At immigration, you won't pay anything at all — it's purely a document check. But the moment you step past that into the airport itself, you'll need a card that works on UK contactless payments, whether you're grabbing a coffee, a snack, or checking into a hostel or hotel. Most accommodation now expects payment by contactless card before or at check-in; cash is accepted in only a limited number of places.

Contactless card is accepted almost everywhere for everyday spending — supermarkets like Tesco, Sainsbury's, Aldi and Lidl, independent shops, and even most street market stalls. Cash is still accepted in plenty of places too, and it's worth carrying a small amount: some independent food stalls and market traders offer a small discount, or round down the price, for customers paying in cash rather than by card.

Where You Might Still Need Cash

Two situations catch visitors out: pay-to-use public toilets and small independent shops. Public toilets are far less common in the UK than in many countries, and where they do charge, it's often a small coin-operated fee — shopping centres are usually free, but standalone facilities at train stations, for example, sometimes require payment before you can enter. It's worth keeping a pound coin or two on hand just in case.

Some smaller independent shops and market stalls also charge a fee or set a minimum spend for card payments, in which case cash is genuinely useful. But for 99% of a typical UK trip, you won't need cash at all.

Getting Around London: Oyster, Contactless and the Tube

As soon as you land, you'll likely be using London's Underground (the Tube) to get into the city, and it runs entirely on card. You'll need a contactless debit or credit card — cash is not accepted at Tube barriers at all. If you'd rather use your phone, simply add your card to Apple Pay or Google Pay before you travel (note that some other mobile wallets are not supported on UK transport readers) and tap in and out exactly as you would with a physical card.

London buses are cashless. There is no option to pay with cash on London's red buses — you must tap a contactless card, phone or Oyster card as you board. Without one of these, you simply won't be able to travel by bus.

Tipping Etiquette in the UK

Tipping in the UK is never mandatory, and it's far less built into the culture than in the US — UK staff are legally paid at least the national minimum wage, so tips top up good service rather than making up a shortfall in pay.

  • Restaurants: check the bill first — many already add a 12.5% "discretionary service charge." If so, no further tip is expected. If not, 10-15% for good service is standard.
  • Pubs and bars: no tip is expected when ordering at the counter.
  • Taxis and rideshare: rounding up the fare, or adding roughly 10% for a longer journey or help with luggage, is common. Nothing is required.
  • Hotels: around £1-2 per bag for porters, and a similar amount per night left for housekeeping, is appreciated but optional.
  • Tour guides: a few pounds per person on a group tour is common; free walking tours in particular rely on tips.

Getting the Best Currency Exchange Rate

If you're converting money before or during your trip, where you do it makes a real difference. Airport currency exchange counters are consistently the worst value, often charging 8-12% more than the mid-market rate — the exact convenience fee you're paying for changing money at the last minute.

Worst Value

Airport bureaux de change and hotel exchange desks — typically 8-12% above the mid-market rate.

Better

High-street banks and bureaux — usually 1.5-3% above the mid-market rate on larger amounts.

Best

Fee-free travel cards (Wise, Revolut, Monzo, Starling) — near mid-market rates for both spending and ATM withdrawals.

The simplest strategy for most trips: spend on a card with no foreign transaction fees, and withdraw cash once from an ATM after arrival if you need some, rather than exchanging a large sum in advance.
