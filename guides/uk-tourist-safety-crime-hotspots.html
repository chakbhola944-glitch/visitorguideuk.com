Travel Safety

Is the UK Safe for Tourists? Real Crime Hotspots, Phone Theft, Pickpocketing & How to Stay Safe

Last Updated: August 2026

A fact-checked look at where tourists in the UK are most likely to experience theft, based on official police and government data — not sensationalised headlines — along with practical steps to protect yourself.

Quick Answer

Yes, the UK is safe for the overwhelming majority of tourists. Violent crime against visitors is rare. The realistic risk is theft — mainly phone snatching and pickpocketing — concentrated in a small number of crowded central London locations, particularly the West End. Official data shows this risk has been falling, and it can be reduced further with simple precautions covered in this guide.

Context First

Understanding UK Crime Statistics

Before looking at any numbers, it helps to understand that "crime" is not one single thing. Official UK statistics separate offences into distinct categories, and mixing them together gives a misleading picture:

  • Theft from the person — items taken directly from someone, such as a phone snatched from their hand, usually without violence.
  • Robbery — theft involving force or the threat of force.
  • Burglary — illegal entry into a building, such as a home or hotel room, to steal.
  • Vehicle offences — theft of or from a car, van or other vehicle.
  • Shoplifting — theft from a shop, which affects retailers rather than individual tourists directly.
  • Phone snatching — a specific, fast-growing form of theft from the person, often involving offenders on bicycles, e-bikes or mopeds.

This article treats each of these separately and always states the exact source, area and time period a figure relates to, rather than presenting one number as if it applies everywhere.

Two important caveats. First, these figures cover England and Wales only — they do not include Scotland or Northern Ireland, which publish their own separate statistics through Police Scotland and the Police Service of Northern Ireland. Second, police-recorded figures reflect reported and recorded crime, not every incident that occurs; the ONS notes that the Crime Survey for England and Wales is generally a better guide to long-term trends, while police data is more useful for tracking specific, well-reported offences like these.

The overall direction across nearly every major theft category was downward in this period. That doesn't mean risk has disappeared, but it contradicts any narrative that crime affecting tourists is simply getting worse everywhere.

Capital City

London: Where Tourists Need to Be Most Alert

London is a huge, globally significant tourist city, and it therefore sees a high volume of reported theft simply because of the sheer number of people passing through it. London is generally a major and well-established tourist destination, but some busy central areas have documented higher levels of theft and phone snatching, and it's worth understanding where and why.

Crime is not evenly spread across the capital — it varies significantly by borough and even by individual street. Westminster, which covers much of the West End, has historically recorded some of the highest levels of theft from the person of any London borough, largely reflecting the sheer footfall of shoppers, commuters and tourists moving through areas like Oxford Street and Leicester Square each day.

There is also genuinely positive, recent, official news to report. In July 2026, the Mayor of London and the Metropolitan Police announced that neighbourhood crime in Westminster had fallen by 36% over the previous 12 months — equivalent to nearly 14,000 fewer offences — following increased police patrols and a new dedicated Command Cell in the West End. Separately, in June 2026, the Met and the Mayor's Office confirmed that mobile phone theft in the West End had fallen by around 50% following a sustained policing crackdown combined with new device security measures introduced with Apple, and that phone theft across London as a whole had fallen by around 13,000 offences over the previous 12 months.

Sources: Metropolitan Police / London City Hall press releases, June–July 2026 (see Sources section below).

These reductions are real and significant, but they do not mean the risk has disappeared. Westminster remains, in the Met's own words, "a national driver of theft from person crimes" — the improvement is a reduction from a high starting point, not an all-clear.

Detailed Example

Oxford Street: One of London's Most Significant Theft Hotspots

Oxford Street is Europe's busiest shopping street, and it has consistently recorded the highest number of phone theft reports of any single London street. Nearby locations including Oxford Circus, Tottenham Court Road, Great Portland Street and Marble Arch are also regularly mentioned in Met Police and Westminster Council communications about phone theft, largely because they sit along the same busy corridor.

In response, Westminster Council, the Metropolitan Police and retailer Currys ran a public awareness campaign called "Mind the Grab," painting purple lines along sections of Oxford Street pavement to remind pedestrians to step back from the kerb and keep phones out of sight — a direct, official acknowledgement of the specific risk on that street.

Tourists can be more vulnerable on Oxford Street for a combination of practical reasons:

  • Extremely crowded pavements make it hard to spot someone approaching quickly.
  • Shopping bags in both hands reduce a visitor's ability to react.
  • Checking a map or directions while walking is a common moment of distraction.
  • Holding a phone up to take photographs leaves it exposed and easy to grab.
  • Stopping suddenly near the kerb to check something puts a phone within reach of the road, where e-bike and moped offenders operate.
  • Crowded Underground station entrances create natural bottlenecks where people are momentarily distracted.

Practical advice: keep your phone in a zipped pocket or bag rather than in your hand while walking along Oxford Street, avoid checking directions near the kerb, and stay aware of cyclists, e-bikes and mopeds moving close to the pavement edge.

Major Risk

Phone Snatching in London: What Tourists Need to Know

Phone snatching is a specific type of theft where an offender grabs a phone directly from someone's hand, rather than reaching into a pocket or bag unnoticed, as with traditional pickpocketing. It's typically fast, opportunistic and over in seconds.

The Metropolitan Police has been explicit that a significant proportion of these offences involve offenders using bicycles, e-bikes, mopeds or motorcycles, allowing them to approach a pedestrian, snatch a visible phone, and escape through traffic before anyone can react. Tourists using their phones near the edge of a pavement or road are particularly exposed to this method.

In 2026, the Met reported that its enforcement operations — including new high-speed e-bikes and drones used to track offenders, alongside a partnership with Apple to make stolen phones harder to resell by rendering them unusable — had contributed to roughly 13,000 fewer phone theft offences across London over the previous 12 months, an 18% year-on-year reduction, with an even sharper 20%+ fall in the first months of 2026 alone.

Source: Metropolitan Police, June 2026 press release on Met and Apple collaboration to reduce phone theft.

This is a genuinely positive trend, not a reason for complacency. London's West End remains a significant national hotspot for this specific crime, and tourists unfamiliar with the local pattern are a natural target simply because they're more likely to be visibly using an unfamiliar phone or map.

Practical Checklist

How to Use a Phone Safely in London

When you need to check Google Maps or directions on a busy street:

  1. Stop walking rather than checking your phone while moving.
  2. Move away from the road or kerb, ideally toward the inside of the pavement.
  3. Stand with your back against a wall or shopfront where practical.
  4. Check the map or directions.
  5. Put the phone away securely before continuing to walk.

Don't

  • Walk along a busy street while holding an expensive phone out in front of you.
  • Stand at the edge of the road while using your phone.
  • Chase a thief if your phone is snatched.
  • Physically fight an offender to keep hold of a device.

Your personal safety is always more important than a phone. No device is worth the risk of injury from a fall, a confrontation, or a moving vehicle.

Crowds & Distraction

Where Pickpocketing Is Most Likely to Be a Problem

Pickpocketing differs from phone snatching in that it typically relies on stealth rather than speed — an offender reaches into a bag, pocket or purse, often in a crowd, without the victim noticing until later. Crowded environments create the cover pickpockets rely on, including:

  • Underground and railway stations, particularly during boarding and alighting
  • Crowded buses
  • Busy shopping streets and markets
  • Popular tourist attractions and their queues
  • Large public events, festivals and celebrations

Reliable, location-specific "top 10" pickpocketing lists for the UK are not consistently published by official sources, so this guide avoids inventing one. The general pattern that does hold up across UK cities is straightforward: risk rises with crowd density and the presence of distracted, unfamiliar visitors, which is precisely the combination found at major stations and attractions.

Practical advice: use a bag with a zip that faces your body, keep valuables in an inside pocket rather than a back pocket or outer bag pocket, and stay aware of your belongings in any crowded queue or carriage.

Sightseeing

Tourist Attractions: Staying Aware While Sightseeing

Tourists are naturally more distracted at attractions than in day-to-day life — taking photographs, reading information boards, buying tickets, entering public transport, or managing luggage all reduce situational awareness for a moment, which is exactly when opportunistic theft can happen.

There is no reliable official data suggesting that any specific major UK attraction is itself a dangerous location, and this guide will not claim otherwise. The advice here is about general behaviour, not any particular site:

  • Keep bags zipped and worn to the front in dense crowds.
  • Avoid leaving a phone or camera unattended on a table or bench, even briefly.
  • Be extra aware while queuing, since queues are a common moment of distraction.
  • Keep a hand on luggage or bags when boarding trains, buses or the Underground.

Crowded Spaces

Markets and Busy Shopping Areas

Crowds create opportunity for theft, and that general risk applies to any busy market or shopping street, including well-known spots like Camden Market, Covent Garden, Borough Market and Portobello Road, as well as the West End shopping district covered earlier in this guide.

Being crowded and popular does not, on its own, make a market a "crime hotspot" — that label should only be used where genuine evidence supports it, which is why this guide singles out Oxford Street specifically rather than markets in general. The sensible approach for any busy market is the same: keep bags secured, stay aware of who is near you, and keep phones out of sight when not actively in use.

Beyond London

Is London the Only UK City With Theft Problems?

No. Theft occurs in every large UK city, and London's high recorded numbers partly reflect its far larger population, visitor numbers and transport volume rather than it being uniquely unsafe. Comparable, tourist-specific theft data isn't published in the same consistent format across every UK police force, so this guide avoids ranking cities against each other — that would go beyond what the evidence actually supports.

What can be said generally is that the same core risks — phone snatching and pickpocketing in crowded central areas, plus vehicle break-ins near attractions and car parks — apply in cities including Manchester, Birmingham, Liverpool, Edinburgh, Glasgow, Leeds, Bristol and Cardiff, in proportion to their size and footfall rather than in some fixed "danger ranking."

Manchester

Main risks: theft from the person and pickpocketing in the busy city centre and around Piccadilly Gardens.

Precaution: keep bags secured on trams and in the Northern Quarter's busiest evenings.

Birmingham

Main risks: theft concentrated around the Bullring and New Street Station, the city's busiest transport hub.

Precaution: stay alert during peak commuter periods around the station.

Liverpool

Main risks: theft in the busy Liverpool ONE shopping area and around the waterfront on event days.

Precaution: keep valuables secure during major events and matchdays, when crowds peak.

Edinburgh

Main risks: pickpocketing on the Royal Mile and around Waverley Station, especially during the Festival Fringe in August.

Precaution: extra vigilance in dense August crowds along the Royal Mile.

Glasgow

Main risks: theft from the person in the central shopping streets and around Buchanan Street.

Precaution: usual bag and phone precautions apply in the busiest retail areas.

Leeds, Bristol & Cardiff

Main risks: theft concentrated around central shopping streets and railway stations, consistent with most UK city centres.

Precaution: standard awareness in crowded areas and near stations.

For current, official, area-specific crime data in any UK city, visitors can check police.uk, which publishes street-level crime maps for every police force in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, or Scotland's equivalent data via Police Scotland.

Rental Cars

Car Theft and Vehicle Crime

Police recorded vehicle offences fell by 13% to 315,923 across England and Wales in the year ending December 2025, continuing a longer-term downward trend in this category.

Source: Office for National Statistics, "Crime in England and Wales: year ending December 2025".

For tourists renting a car, the main risk is theft of visible items left inside the vehicle rather than the car itself. Practical precautions:

  • Never leave luggage visible in a rental car, even briefly.
  • Do not leave passports, cameras or electronics inside the vehicle.
  • Always lock the car, even for short stops.
  • Park in well-lit, secure locations where practical, such as staffed car parks.
  • Follow the specific security instructions provided by your rental company.
  • Remember that a locked car is not a guarantee against opportunistic theft of visible items.

Accommodation Security

Burglary and Accommodation Security

Burglary means unauthorised entry into a building with intent to steal, and it doesn't necessarily involve smashing a window — unlocked doors or ground-floor windows are common entry points. Police recorded burglary fell 12% to 224,518 offences across England and Wales in the year ending December 2025.

Tourists staying in hotels, serviced apartments, hostels or short-term rentals should apply the same normal security precautions they would at home:

  • Lock doors and windows whenever you leave, even for a short time.
  • Don't leave valuables visible from a window or balcony.
  • Use a hotel safe where one is provided, particularly for passports and spare cards.
  • Avoid unnecessarily advertising expensive possessions, such as leaving designer bags or electronics in plain view.

Watches & Jewellery

Can Someone Steal a Watch or Jewellery From a Tourist?

Robbery involving watches and jewellery does occur, and there have been high-profile cases in London specifically targeting expensive watches. This is a real, if statistically uncommon, risk rather than a reason to avoid wearing normal jewellery.

A balanced approach: an everyday watch or simple jewellery is not something you need to hide or leave at home. Very expensive, easily recognisable luxury watches or jewellery can draw unwanted attention in crowded public spaces, so it's sensible to avoid displaying them unnecessarily, particularly at night or in unfamiliar areas, without needing to feel unsafe wearing a normal watch throughout your trip.

Action Plan

If Your Phone Is Stolen

  1. Move somewhere safe, away from the immediate area.
  2. Do not chase the offender.
  3. Report the crime to police — call 999 if it just happened and the offender may still be nearby, or use 101 or the police's online reporting tool for a non-emergency report afterwards.
  4. Use Find My iPhone (Apple) or Find My Device (Google/Android) to lock, track or erase the phone remotely.
  5. Contact your mobile network provider to suspend the SIM and account.
  6. Block the SIM or account if the network provider hasn't already done so.
  7. Change passwords for any important accounts accessible from the phone.
  8. Contact your bank if the phone had access to banking apps or saved card details.
  9. Keep the police crime reference number — you'll need it for insurance.
  10. Contact your travel insurance provider to begin a claim if you're covered.

Action Plan

If Your Passport or Bag Is Stolen

  • Report to police and obtain a crime reference number, either in person, via 101, or through online reporting.
  • Contact your embassy or consulate in the UK to begin emergency travel document arrangements.
  • Contact your travel insurance provider to check what's covered and begin a claim.
  • Cancel and replace bank cards if they were in the stolen bag.
  • Arrange replacement travel documents through your embassy before your planned departure date, allowing extra time for processing.

Safety Reminders

Never Do These Things

A Quick Reference

  • Don't chase a phone thief.
  • Don't fight a robber.
  • Don't stand next to the road using your phone.
  • Don't leave your bag unattended.
  • Don't leave luggage visible in a car.
  • Don't keep your passport in an open backpack pocket.
  • Don't display large amounts of cash.
  • Don't assume tourist areas are completely risk-free.

Emergency Information

UK Emergency Numbers

999

Emergency police, ambulance or fire — use when there is immediate danger or a crime is in progress.

101

Non-emergency police — use to report a crime that has already happened and is not currently an emergency.

Crimes can also be reported online through the relevant police force's official website — for example the Metropolitan Police at met.police.uk for London, or the appropriate local force elsewhere in the UK.

The Bigger Picture

Don't Let Statistics Stop You From Visiting

Don't let crime statistics stop you from visiting the UK. Instead, understand where the risks are and take simple precautions. Millions of tourists visit the UK every year, and the overwhelming majority complete their trip without becoming a victim of any crime, let alone a serious one.

The goal of this guide is awareness, not fear. Knowing that Oxford Street sees a lot of phone snatching, that Westminster is a genuine national hotspot for theft from the person, and that simple habits — putting your phone away while walking, keeping bags zipped, not leaving valuables in a parked car — meaningfully reduce your risk, lets you enjoy your trip with confidence rather than anxiety.
